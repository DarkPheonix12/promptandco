  **llms.txt in 2026** — a map file that the citation engines barely fetch. In Ahrefs'
  137,000-domain audit, 97% of published files got zero requests in a month, and the AI
  retrieval bots that actually decide citations were 1.1% of the little traffic that
  existed. The rare readers were coding agents (Claude-Code at the top) and training
  crawlers (GPTBot) — infrastructure and future-proofing, not a visibility lever.

In the [11-levers model](/resources/research/ai-citation-levers-2026), llms.txt sits where
schema markings sit after the controlled evidence got in: real, cheap, and read by the wrong
readers for the job you probably wanted it for. This article is the dedicated, evidence-led
treatment — the sibling verdict to [schema's null result](/resources/research/schema-ai-citations-2026-evidence),
now with the largest log dataset we have on who actually fetches the file.

## One number changed the debate

**97%.** That is the share of published llms.txt files that received **zero requests of any
kind** in May 2026 — no bots, no humans, nothing. The number comes from Ahrefs' analysis of
**137,210 domains** (Linehan & Guan, published June 2026), and it turned the loudest single
file in AI search from a "must-have 2026 tactic" into a measured question.

The paradox that makes the number interesting: adoption is exploding. The same 28% of that
technical-skewed panel publish the file — roughly 38,000 domains — and Originality.ai's
tracker shows instances grew **8.8x in twelve months** (4,088 → 36,120 by May 2026, 38,980
including llms-full.txt and ai.txt). More sites than ever are publishing a file that almost
nobody requests. That is a signal about *who is selling* the tactic, not about what engines
read.

## What the 137,000-site audit actually measured

The methodology matters, because it is the cleanest look at this file to date. Ahrefs took
every domain in its Web Analytics panel that received traffic in May 2026, checked each root
for an `llms.txt` returning HTTP 200, then used Bot Analytics to classify **every request**
to the `/llms.txt` path — split by HTTP response (200 vs 404) and by individual user agent.

Two consequences shape every other number in this article:

- **"Fetched" is not "read".** Many bots pull the file and never act on it. Every figure
  below is therefore a *ceiling* on actual llms.txt consumption — real usage is at or below
  what the logs show.
- **99.9% of the story is the null.** Of ~38,000 valid files, 97% drew nothing at all. All
  llms.txt traffic in the study came from ~1,100 domains and about 22,000 requests.

## Who fetches the file: the inverted pyramid

When the file *is* fetched, the reader list is the opposite of what its promoters assumed.
96% of requests came from bots, but **77% of those bots are not AI tools at all** — and the
AI bots that did show up are the wrong AI bots:

| Reader category | Share of requests | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SEO audit tools | ~21.7% | The industry studying itself; Ahrefs' own crawlers included |
| **AI agents & agentic infra** | **10.5%** | Claude-Code top of *all* AI bots; coding agents, not search |
| AI training crawlers | 5.3% | GPTBot alone 4.51%; ClaudeBot 0.80%; DeepseekBot 0.02% |
| Discoverability / validator bots | 3.6% | Audit the standard, not the site |
| AI assistants | ~2% | Consumer chatbot surfaces |
| **AI retrieval bots** | **1.1%** | OAI-SearchBot, PerplexityBot — the bots that decide citations |

The comparison that lands it: **Slackbot — the chat link-unfurl bot — requested llms.txt
more often than PerplexityBot did.** And the negative result is equally clean: AI bots never
go looking for files that don't exist. Split by HTTP response, missing files drew 98% human
traffic (SEOs checking competitors) and the AI-bot share of those 404s was **zero**. Even
the Chrome Lighthouse audit that reignited the debate produced roughly **1 in 1,000** of all
requests. The taxonomies line up: AI agents are a real audience and AI search retrieval is
not — yet.

## Four independent methods, the same null

A single null can replicate badly; four different designs agreeing is different. Alongside
Ahrefs' server logs:

- **SE Ranking (Nov 2025)** modeled ~300,000 domains — of which about 10% had the file — and
  found **no correlation between llms.txt and AI-citation frequency**. They then built a
  gradient-boosted prediction model to catch a weak signal; removing the llms.txt feature
  *improved* the model's accuracy. The variable was behaving as noise.
- **OtterlyAI (90 days, 62,100+ AI bot visits)** tracked what crawlers actually hit: the
  `/llms.txt` path received 84 visits — roughly **3x worse than an average content page**.
- **EZY Research (12 weeks, 83 sites with live logs)** compared robots.txt and llms.txt
  fetches on the same estate: OpenAI 3,990 vs **7**; Anthropic 3,120 vs **9**; PerplexityBot
  775 vs **0**; Googlebot 5,125 vs 67. The one counter-intuitive datapoint: **Meta's
  Meta-ExternalAgent read llms.txt (193) more often than it read robots.txt (172)** — the
  only major AI company currently consuming the file at scale.
- **A 900-domain, seven-month server-log study** saw just 1,227 family-path requests total,
  with 88% of the estate never requested once and **zero verified frontier-lab crawlers**
  among the readers.

Methodological convergence is the point: logs, a predictive citation model, crawl tracking,
and a second log study all land in the same place. There is no well-populated citation
pathway through llms.txt today.

## Why the file cannot move citations — structurally

Robots.txt and llms.txt look like siblings and behave differently. **Robots.txt is a control
file every crawler checks on every crawl** — that is why it drew thousands of fetches in the
same logs where llms.txt drew single digits. **llms.txt is an optional, opt-in map:** agents
fetch it *when directed*, not speculatively, and no AI system penalizes you for not having
one. Publishing cannot put you on any AI radar, because nothing goes looking for it.

The platform record agrees:

- **Google** — the AI-search guide's MYTHBUSTING section states machine-readable files like
  llms.txt are **not required** for generative-AI features and carry no signal; **John
  Mueller** has called the file "not done for search" — "a temporary crutch, perhaps to save
  some tokens" for AI coding tools. The only Google property touching it is **Chrome
  Lighthouse**, which added an llms.txt audit on **May 7, 2026** — filed under a new
  *agentic browsing* category, not the SEO audit group. Google's own taxonomy says agent
  tooling, not search optimisation.
- **OpenAI / Anthropic / Perplexity** — crawler documentation points to **robots.txt**;
  llms.txt appears in their own developer-docs as a lightweight map. No major engine has
  committed to reading it for ranking, retrieval, or citations.

So the honest label is *future-readiness infrastructure*: a standard that could matter
through the agent layer, that no citation engine currently weights.

## Where the file genuinely matters in 2026

The one audience with a documented, real use case: **coding agents and developer tooling.**
Claude-Code out-fetched every AI retrieval bot, assistant, and training crawler except
GPTBot; Cursor, GitHub Copilot, Continue, and Aider read the file to navigate documentation
sites; Mintlify's internal data shows agents reach for llms-full.txt at more than twice the
rate of the standard file. If your customers run coding agents, or agents act on your site
on their behalf, a clean llms.txt stands a real chance of being read.

Two supporting arguments keep it rational:

- **The WebMCP horizon.** Google's weight is not on llms.txt — it is on WebMCP (the Web Model
  Context Protocol), structured agent-to-site tool contracts in HTML, shipping in an origin
  trial in Chrome 149, alongside the A2A protocol announced in April 2025. llms.txt is
  scaffolding the agent layer may standardize around; it is optionality, not today's
  visibility.
- **Common Crawl's July 2026 crawl** found that even when the file exists, quality varies
  enormously: of 6.5M sampled URLs, 69.8% returned 404, and under half of the 200s carried a
  text body. Validated files carry policy language and — in a subset — **instructions
  addressed to the model itself**. Agents trust ingested content, which is why prompt
  injection in auto-generated files is a real audit finding.

## The decision framework

1. **Developer-docs / API / SDK site → publish, and do it properly.** Link the file from
   your HTML and docs (agents fetch when directed), keep redirects and status clean (the
   Lighthouse audit only fails on a server error), and keep the map accurate, not marketing.
2. **Marketing, e-commerce, and local sites → optional.** If a platform hands you the file,
   keep it. Do not spend budget on it expecting AI citations.
3. **If you publish, do it right:** follow the spec (llmstxt.org) so validators parse it,
   keep it a map of the site rather than a pitch, keep it current, and do not bake prompt
   instructions into it.
4. **Measure from your own logs.** Your server logs are the only test that settles it for
   your site — and the base rate is that a 97% chance nothing reads it in any given month is
   the default, not a bug. The
   [measurement buyer's guide](/resources/research/ai-visibility-measurement-buyers-guide)
   covers how to track named AI mentions without fooling yourself.

## Bottom line

llms.txt is a real file with a real — and narrow — audience: **coding agents, developer
docs, and future agent infrastructure.** It is not, on any 2026 dataset, a citation lever.
Publish it as cheap insurance if it is free or your buyers run agents; make sure your
crawl-access and content-entity layers are genuinely working; and spend the citation budget
on the levers the engines have shown they read — the entity, trust, and evidence work covered
in the [11-levers hub](/resources/research/ai-citation-levers-2026). Your own server logs are
the tiebreaker, and the base rate is on your side: you are not doing anything wrong when the
file stays quiet.
