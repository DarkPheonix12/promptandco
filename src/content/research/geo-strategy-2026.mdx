If you have read [What Is GEO](/resources/research/what-is-generative-engine-optimization),
you know what generative engine optimization is and why it exists. This page is the next question:
**what do you actually do about it, in what order, and how do you know it is working?**

The honest answer is that most GEO programs fail not because the tactics are wrong but because the
**order is wrong** — they start with content or PR before they have a baseline or a technical foundation,
and then they have no reliable way to tell whether anything changed. This page is the corrective: a
strategy framework in priority order, with the reasoning for each step.

## The five-layer GEO strategy

A GEO program has five layers. They are listed in priority order, because the layers build on each other
and skipping a lower layer makes the layers above it noisy or pointless.

### Layer 1 — Baseline and prompt panel

**What it is.** A fixed set of buying and research prompts — the questions your buyers actually ask AI
systems when evaluating your category — run consistently across the engines that matter, with a recorded
method for how often each is run and how results are logged.

**Why it comes first.** You cannot improve what you do not measure, and AI visibility is not visible in the
way rankings are. A baseline is the only way to know whether a change moved the needle or whether the answer
just changed because the model updated. The methodology literature recommends running each query multiple times
per session and re-measuring on two-to-four-week windows, because single-run numbers are noisy and repeated runs
of the same prompt can show source overlap as low as 32–43%.

**What it produces.** A documented starting point: which brands are named, which are cited, which are absent,
and what facts are stated about each — across a defined panel, on a defined cadence. This is the number
everything else is measured against.

**The trap.** Treating a one-off audit as a baseline. An audit is a snapshot; AI answers change faster than
most audits are read. A baseline is a method, not an event.

### Layer 2 — Technical access for AI crawlers

**What it is.** Ensuring that the raw HTML served to crawlers contains the content you want retrieved, that
robots.txt is not blocking the crawlers you want to be visible to, that structured data is valid, and that
pages render for agents the way they do for humans.

**Why it comes second.** If an AI crawler cannot read your page, nothing else matters. OtterlyAI's analysis
of a million AI citations found that roughly 73% of sites carried at least one technical barrier that could
prevent citation — primarily client-side rendering that hides content from non-browser clients. This is the
highest-impact, lowest-glamour layer, and it is the one most often skipped.

**What it produces.** A crawlable, renderable, parseable site — the prerequisite for every other layer.

**The trap.** Assuming that because Google can render your page, AI crawlers can too. Many AI crawlers do not
execute JavaScript the way a browser does, and client-side rendering is the single most common citation barrier.

### Layer 3 — Entity foundation

**What it is.** Making the brand's identity — name, description, offering, locations, people, services —
consistent and machine-readable across the site, schema and external sources, and resolving the conflicts that
confuse systems.

**Why it comes third.** AI systems organize the world as entities — distinct things with attributes and
relationships — not as keyword strings. If your brand's entity is thin, inconsistent or conflated with other
companies, you are harder to retrieve and riskier to recommend. Entity work is also what makes the content
restructuring in Layer 4 land on the right concept rather than a near-miss.

**What it produces.** A clear, consistent, machine-readable entity: an Organization with a real description, a
logo, valid sameAs where profiles exist, Service schema for what you do, and Person schema for the people behind
it — plus consistency between what the site says and what external sources say.

**The trap.** Treating entity optimization as a schema plugin. Schema helps, but the entity signal is also in
what the content says, what external sources say, and whether the version of your company on your site matches
the version on your LinkedIn, your Crunchbase, your press mentions and your reviews.

### Layer 4 — Content restructuring for extraction

**What it is.** Rewriting the priority pages — the ones that answer the questions and prompts that matter — into
extractable, source-worthy form: clear headings, direct answers near the top, lists, tables, FAQ blocks, how-to
structure, and honest depth.

**Why it comes fourth.** Structured content is what engines retrieve and quote. Controlled experiments found that
adding FAQ structured data, statistics, named expert sources and citations to content increased visibility in
AI-generated answers by meaningful margins, while keyword stuffing reduced it. But structure without substance is
thin content, and thin content is not what engines cite.

**What it produces.** A smaller library of original, well-structured, expert-led pages that engines can retrieve,
quote and cite — starting with the pages that answer the prompts in your panel.

**The trap.** Publishing more pages instead of fixing the right ones. Volume without substance dilutes entity and
authority signals. The evidence favors a smaller library of stronger pages.

### Layer 5 — Third-party footprint and citation building

**What it is.** Mapping the sources AI systems actually cite for your category — listicles, review platforms,
directories, publications — and building presence there. This is where digital PR, review-platform work and
comparison content enter.

**Why it comes fifth.** For commercial and recommendation queries, AI answers are heavily populated by third-party
content. DerivateX found that roughly 63% of the AI-cited sources in its B2B topic set were listicles,
comparisons and review-platform content. You cannot only build your own pages; you also need to be named,
correctly and credibly, in the formats and sources the engines cite for your category. But this layer is also the
most expensive and the slowest, which is why it comes after the layers that are under your direct control.

**What it produces.** Presence in the sources your category's engines cite — earned, not claimed — and a citation
footprint that compounds over time against the baseline.

**The trap.** Buying links or farming mentions. AI systems are increasingly good at detecting manipulation, and
beyond the ethics, it does not last. The legitimate version of this layer is slow, real, and built on things worth
citing — original research, expert commentary, honest comparison content, and the kind of third-party presence that
would matter even if AI did not exist.

## The order matters more than the tactics

The most common expensive GEO mistake is doing Layer 5 before Layer 1 — hiring a PR firm or a content mill to
"build AI visibility" before anyone has measured where the brand stands or whether the site is even readable by
the crawlers that would cite it.

The second most common mistake is doing Layer 4 at scale before Layer 3 — publishing a large library of
restructured content when the entity signal is thin or inconsistent, so the content lands on the wrong concept or
does not connect to a verifiable brand.

The least common but most costly mistake is believing a guarantee. No one can guarantee what a third-party model
generates. Any engagement that promises guaranteed AI placement is selling certainty that does not exist, and the
agencies that promise it are the ones you should not trust with anything.

The right order is: measure, fix access, clarify the entity, restructure the priority content, then build the
third-party footprint — and re-measure on a fixed cadence so you know whether the work moved the distribution.

## What a GEO strategy looks like by category

The five layers are universal. The weight each layer carries depends on the category.

### SaaS

A SaaS GEO strategy weights Layer 4 and Layer 5 heavily — review platforms, comparison content, documentation,
and the kind of expert-led content engines cite for software evaluation queries. The citation patterns for SaaS
are well-studied: third-party listicles and comparison content dominate, and pricing-answer stacks — a
server-rendered pricing page, natural-language FAQs, billing documentation — are what get cited for pricing queries.
Layer 2 matters if the product docs or pricing are client-side rendered. Layer 3 matters because SaaS brands are
often conflated with competitors or with the category itself.

### B2B professional services

A B2B professional services GEO strategy weights Layer 3 and Layer 5 — expert entities, analyst-style content,
third-party presence and the kind of authority signals that make a firm citable as a source rather than just a
vendor. The citation patterns favor named experts and authoritative third-party mentions over volume. Layer 1 is
especially important here because B2B buying prompts are specific and the panel must reflect the actual questions
buyers ask, not generic category terms.

### E-commerce

An e-commerce GEO strategy weights Layer 2 and Layer 4 — technical access to product data, structured data for
products and reviews, and content that answers the evaluation questions buyers ask AI systems. The citation patterns
for product queries skew to product pages, reviews and third-party retail coverage. Layer 5 matters but looks
different: retail press, reviewer coverage and the review platforms shoppers actually use.

### Local services

A local-services GEO strategy weights Layer 3 and a version of Layer 5 that is dominated by business profiles,
reviews and local entity signals — Google Business Profile, the consistent NAP and category data that local search
and AI systems use to verify a business, and the review platforms local buyers consult. The citation patterns for
local queries are heavily local-entity-driven. Layer 1 is important but the panel must include the geo-modified
prompts local buyers actually use.

## Measurement — the part most strategies skip

The hardest part of GEO is not doing the work. It is measuring whether the work mattered.

Traditional SEO has position tracking. GEO has something harder: being named or not in an answer that changes with
model updates, user context and prompt phrasing. The right measurement is a panel run consistently over time, with
the same prompts, the same engines, the same methodology — and the right question is whether the distribution is
moving, not whether a single run looked good.

The practical measurement stack:

- **A fixed prompt panel**, defined up front, covering buying prompts, research prompts and comparison prompts
  for the category.
- **A run cadence**, monthly at minimum, weekly for active engagements — because citation presence decays on a
  timescale of weeks.
- **The right KPIs**, which are distributions not points: share of answer, citation sources, sentiment and accuracy,
  and the presence-or-absence question for the prompts that matter.
- **A review loop**, where movement — or the lack of it — feeds back into the work.

This is also why ongoing monitoring is part of a real program, not an optional add-on. An agency or in-house team
that measures once and stops is not measuring the thing it claims to optimize.

## The tools layer — measurement, not execution

The GEO tools landscape in 2026 is a measurement layer, not an execution layer. The players that have real traction
include Profound — the category leader for agency-mode brand and prompt tracking — along with Evertune, Scrunch AI,
Peec AI and OtterlyAI, each with different engine coverage, prompt-tracking depth and pricing. The incumbents —
Semrush, Ahrefs — are adding AI-search features on top of their existing index.

The honest line: **tools measure, humans execute.** A tool will tell you where you stand; it will not fix your
rendering, restructure your content, or earn you citations in the sources your category's answers are built from.
Many real engagements pair a tool for measurement with a human program for the work. The right call depends on
whether you need the measurement layer, the execution layer, or both.

## What a GEO strategy does not include

A real GEO strategy is explicit about what it does not promise.

- **Guaranteed placement.** No one controls what a third-party model generates.
- **A substitute for SEO.** Crawlability, indexability, relevance and technical health are still prerequisites.
- **A one-time project.** Citation presence decays on a timescale of weeks; a strategy without a measurement cadence
  is not a strategy.
- **A single tactic.** Schema helps, but it is one input among many. The citation datasets show that third-party
  presence and source authority matter at least as much for commercial queries.
- **Volume without substance.** Thin content is not what engines cite. The evidence favors a smaller library of
  stronger pages.
