  **Freshness** — the measurable tendency of answer engines and AI-assisted search to
  prefer content that shows recent, real update signals. In large citation samples the gap
  is decisive: pages updated inside the last month are associated with roughly **3.2x** the
  citation share of pages a year or older. Freshness is not a tax you pay to search engines —
  it is a property of the *evidence pool* that assistants are allowed to cite.
</DefinitionCard>

In the [2026 AI citation study](/resources/research/2026-ai-citation-study) we flagged
"recently updated wins" as one of the few levers with strong cross-engine evidence. This
article is the working version of that finding: the underlying data, the mechanisms that
make recency matter inside answer engines, and a tiered update system you can run on an
existing archive without breaking what already works.

## The evidence: how large, how measured

The cleanest public number comes from **ConvertMate's January 2026 analysis of 105,000
articles** tracked over time. The headline relationships, normalized across categories:

| Last updated | Relative citation share |
| --- | --- |
| Within the last ~30 days | **3.2x** baseline |
| 1–3 months ago | ~2x |
| 3–12 months ago | ~1.3x |
| 1–2 years ago | ~1x (baseline) |
| 2+ years ago | ~0.85x |

Read it as an association, not a causal guarantee: sites that update frequently also tend
to do other things well. But the size of the gradient, in a large longitudinal sample, is
why freshness keeps appearing in citation research.

Two complementary signals reinforce it. Trakkr's studies of answer-engine behaviour found
roughly **73.5% of AI-search sessions end in the first answer** — if your page is the
source that snapshot is built from, you win the session; recency materially influences which
page the snapshot is built from. And the retrieval research we covered in the
[crawler playbook](/resources/research/ai-crawlers-2026-readiness) shows assistants budget
limited fetches per query and favour content that behaves like it is active.

## Why recency works inside answer engines

Three mechanisms, roughly in order of how much they matter:

- **Recency-biased snapshot selection.** When an engine assembles an answer, it can draw its
  source snapshot from any candidate page that matches. Fresh pages that still carry the
  exact evidence win because the retrieval layer considers recency a ranking input for
  queries with temporal intent — and a distressingly large share of commercial queries have
  temporal intent.
- **The evidence-pool copy problem.** Assistants copy and cache source text with the answer.
  Yesterday's page, cached, is what gets quoted; today's correction or improvement only
  enters the answer when the agent re-fetches the URL. A refresh cadence is cheap insurance
  that your current best version — not last quarter's — is what gets cited.
- **The back-fill effect.** Answer engines re-run and re-fetch frequently asked questions.
  A page that visibly updates invites re-verification; a page that has not changed in
  eighteen months reads as stale to the same machinery, even when the content is timeless.

## A tiered update system that respects your archive

You do not need to rewrite your whole site monthly. You need a cadence engine tied to how
much a topic actually changes:

**Tier 1 — Money and trend-dependent pages (every 2–4 weeks).** Pricing, methodology,
comparisons, compatibility matrices, "best X" lists, and any page whose search demand
moves with trends. Update the facts, dates, and examples that drift; verify numbers; add a
visible edit note where useful.

**Tier 2 — Evergreen reference (quarterly to half-yearly).** Definitions, fundamentals,
and long-lived explainers. The job is *verification*: read it, check links and numbers
still hold, update the timestamp honestly only if you changed content.

**Tier 3 — News and data pages (continuous).** Where you own a dataset or a timeline (our
[research index](/resources/research) lives here), append entries as they occur. Continuous
artifacts like this are the most citation-friendly content class that exists, because every
update hands the engine legitimate new evidence on a stable URL.

For each tier the discipline is identical:

1. **Edit, then date — never just date.** A timestamp you cannot justify is a liability in
   both directions: engines that distrust it, and the missed re-fetch you were hoping to
   trigger. Google's own guidance has consistently been to update old URLs in place rather
   than move or recreate them, precisely because the signals live on the URL.
2. **Preserve what got you cited.** Before you touch a page, note the specific facts and
   quotes that are likely in answer snapshots. Refresh around them. Rewriting away your
   cited evidence is the single most common way sites convert a good freshness program into
   a citation regression.
3. **Repoint internal links at the refreshed asset.** When you improve a page, tell the
   rest of the site — and the crawlers doing the re-fetch — by linking to it from your
   [sitemap](/sitemap.xml) and your most-linked article.
4. **Measure on citation share, not traffic.** Track the 30-day window: which pages appear
   as sources or are quoted in assistants, before and after a refresh. Traffic and organic
   rankings lag and lie; the citation window is the honest scoreboard.
5. **Keep a platform changelog.** If your team publishes a lightweight "what changed this
   week" list for materially updated pages, you create an internal source-of-truth that
   also happens to be engine-friendly evidence.

## What honesty looks like in practice

We have deliberately not invented a "perfect cadence" number here, because the cross-engine
controlled experiment that would prove one does not exist yet — it is exactly the kind of
measurement our upcoming **Edition 01 panel** is built to run. What the available evidence
supports is directional: pages active on roughly **two-to-four-week** cycles outperform
inactive pages, month-old content beats year-old content by a wide margin, and
truthful, verifiable updates behave better than cosmetic date bumps in every dataset we
have seen. If you implement nothing else: pick ten pages that matter, give each a real edit
every month, and watch whether their citation footprint moves in two cycles. That is the
whole playbook, and it is honest enough to run your business on.
