In the [11-levers model](/resources/research/ai-citation-levers-2026), schema (lever 9)
sits in the *openly contested* column, and this article explains exactly why. The evidence
story of 2026 is a story of a correlation that survived, a causal effect that did not, and
a nuance — attribute-rich vs generic markup — that most of the conversation still misses.
No other claim in this series has produced such a complete stack of controlled tests
pointing one direction, so it deserves the full treatment.

## The correlation everyone quotes — and the trap hidden in it

Start with the number that created the "schema for AI" industry. In a May 2026 analysis of
**6 million URLs**, Ahrefs found AI-cited pages carry JSON-LD about **three times more
often** than non-cited pages (roughly 53% vs 19%; Linehan & Guan, 2026). An independent
corpus worker in the same period — AirOps analyzed 16,851 queries — found JSON-LD pages
cited at 38.5% vs 32.0% for pages without it (a +6.5-point gap; Indig, 2026). The peer-
reviewed side agrees: Schanbacher's study of 1,508 German real-estate sites found FAQPage
(OR ≈ 13, p < .001) and Product (OR ≈ 4) schema associated with ChatGPT visibility.

Three sources, three different designs, one consistent correlation. It is real. It is also
predictable, for one boring reason: **the sites that implement schema are the sites that
also implement everything else.** Schema is the visible tip of a site managed by a competent
team that also writes good content, earns links, and maintains the technical layer. The
correlation exists because the *practice* of doing schema and the *practice* of getting
cited share a common cause. Even the famous GEO-16 structured-data finding — r = 0.63 and a
"+39% citation impact" figure that vendors still quote — inherits a fundamental flaw: its
corpus was harvested *from* citations, with no uncited population to compare against. The
+39% is an over-quoted artifact of sampling, not a measurement of cause.

## The 2026 controlled evidence: adding schema changes nothing

The reason these correlations don't settle it is that 2026 finally produced the
experiments. All of them ran on matched, controlled designs — and all of them came back
near zero.

- **Ahrefs' difference-in-differences RCT (May 2026).** After the correlational audit,
  the researchers tracked **1,885 pages that added JSON-LD** against ~4,000 matched
  controls from August 2025 to March 2026, measuring citations across Google AI Overviews,
  AI Mode, and ChatGPT. Results: AI Mode +2.4% (statistically indistinguishable from
  zero), ChatGPT +2.2% (indistinguishable from zero), AI Overviews **−4.6%** (small but
  statistically significant — a decline). Four separate tests, same direction: schema did
  not move citations for pages AI already had access to. The pages were not new or
  invisible; they were mid- to high-visibility pages where markup was the only variable.
- **The GEO Lab FAQPage experiment (480 queries).** A controlled side-by-side found FAQPage
  markup at **−1.7%** and, more importantly, exposed the mention-vs-citation gap: Gemini
  *mentioned* a marked-up test page 21.2% of the time yet *cited* it **0%** (Ferreira,
  2026). The graph is consulted; the page is not linked.
- **Authority Radar's 90-day FAQ study (August 2026).** 120 matched pages, ~12,000 prompt-
  runs: ChatGPT +0.1pp (null), AI Mode/OAO +0.1pp (null), Gemini −0.5pp (null). The single
  exception was Perplexity at **+1.3pp** — modest, at a 90% confidence interval, but real.
  Every null result to date has held; Perplexity is the only engine where FAQ markup
  measurably helps.
- **Fischman's corrected models (SSRN 6284518, Feb 2026).** The largest single citation
  corpus around schema (730 observed citations across ChatGPT and Gemini) initially looked
  negative — pooled OR 0.546, p < .001 — but the effect turned out to be an artifact: the
  citations inherited Google's rank bias. Corrected for retrieval position, schema presence
  (OR 0.678), entity richness (OR 1.001) and alignment (OR 1.068) all go null, while
  position dominates: OR 0.762 per position, 43% citation at position 1 collapsing to 5% by
  position 7. **Rank is the lever; markup is not.**
- **searchVIU's live-retrieval test.** Five AI systems — ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity,
  Gemini, and AI Mode — all extract only visible HTML when selecting a citation source;
  in-page JSON-LD, Microdata, and RDFa are ignored at that step. Williams-Cook's planted
  tests reached the same place differently: engines read malformed JSON-LD as ordinary
  *text* (DUCKYEA test), which is why "your schema is broken" rarely breaks retrieval and
  "your schema is perfect" rarely improves it.

The institutional column backs the experiments. Google's first official AI-search doc
("MYTHBUSTING") says flatly that schema.org markup is **not required** for AI features, and
the FAQ rich-results program — the last prominent *visible* payoff of FAQPage markup — was
fully sunset on **May 7, 2026**.

## The nuance that survives: attribute-rich markup and visible facts

If markup were uniformly worthless, that would be the whole story. It isn't. The controlled
data supports a finer claim: **facts win; markup format mostly doesn't matter; and
attribute-rich markup correlates with facts-rich pages.**

In Fischman's corpus, pages carrying Product or Review schema with *populated* prices,
ratings, and attributes were cited at **61.7%**, versus 41.6% for generic types. Then the
critical detail that almost everyone reporting this skipped: pages with **no schema at all
sat at 59.8%** — statistically indistinguishable from the rich-markup group (as verified by
Daniel Cheung's June 2026 re-analysis of the full paper). Rich schema ≈ no schema; generic
schema was the actual loser. citability.dev's per-type tracking across three engines
agrees with the ordering — FAQPage 71%, HowTo 66%, Article 58%, TechArticle 55% — and with
the "*which fields are filled*" point: populated `author`, `dateModified`, and `headline`
matter more than which type you choose (Haller, 2026). Perplexity weights
SoftwareApplication and Product; Claude's citability runs hot on FAQ content.

Two places where markup is genuinely read rather than ignored:

- **Bing's LLMs.** Schema is *documented* as helpful for Copilot and for Bing's side of
  the ChatGPT index (Capconvert's synthesis of Bing's Canel's guidance, 2026) — the one
  agent pipeline where a schema-positive statement survives scrutiny.
- **The training layer, not the retrieval layer.** Engine pre-training draws on the Web
  Data Commons' extracted RDF — 106B quads from ~3.1B entities — so high-quality markup on
  high-quality pages feeds the *parametric* memory that later gets recited as "knowledge."
  That is a long-horizon brand-facts channel, distinct from (and slower than) live citation.

And then there is the inference the correlated data drags in as a rider: aggregateRating
and review presence behave like *corroboration*, not markup. In Ahrefs' companion audit,
a brand with no review profile got cited **1%** of the time; one actively collecting
reviews, **75.3%** — while review platforms alone supply ~14% of all citations. That is the
real [entity and trust wiring](/resources/research/eeat-ai-trust-signals-2026) doing
work that gets mis-attributed to schema.

## So where does markup still pay in 2026? Five uses

Honest bottom line. Schema is not an AI-citation *growth* lever, but it is not dead. It
pays in five specific places — each labeled for what it is:

1. **Entity and disambiguation infrastructure.** The `sameAs` array and Organization
   `@id` spine are a merge instruction telling engines your profiles describe one entity.
   This is the [entity layer](/resources/research/entity-optimization-2026), not a
   citation lever — but it is the layer everything else needs.
2. **Training-corpus brand facts.** Consistent, populated markup feeds Web Data Commons and,
   eventually, parametric recall. Slow, compounding, invisible in any 3-month window —
   hygiene, not a switch (Web Data Commons, 2026).
3. **Bing / Copilot compatibility.** The one documented live-retrieval consumer that reads
   schema. Cheap insurance; zero downside; real (Capconvert, 2026).
4. **Last-mile attribute precision on pages already cited.** On content that clears
   retrieval, populated price/rating/spec attributes give an answer engine exact quotable
   facts instead of forcing it to paraphrase. This is where attribute-rich markup arguably
   edges visible text — a real but *marginal* effect, and only on pages that would be
   cited anyway. (Practitioner-standard; no controlled study isolates it.)
5. **Visible facts first, markup second.** The 59.8%-vs-61.7% result means you get almost
   all the benefit by writing the facts into the page in plain prose. Markup is a
   transcript of facts in a machine-traceable shape, and a copy of that transcript in the
   training layer — it is never a substitute for the fact.

Yes — that includes every page in this research series, which carries BlogPosting and
FAQPage JSON-LD. It's last-mile SEO infrastructure and semantic context, not a citation
trick. Writing this article didn't make this article citable; writing *evidence* will,
which is the point.

## The reallocation rule

If you budget schema as an AI-citation strategy, 2026 says you have it backwards. The
controlled stack — Ahrefs' RCT, the GEO Lab's FAQ test, Authority Radar's 90-day run,
Fischman's position-corrected models, searchVIU's retrieval tests — agrees on three
decisions:

- **Keep schema, but as hygiene.** One Organization block, a complete `sameAs` array, one
  FAQ block on genuinely varied questions, populated attributes where facts exist. Budget
  it like server monitoring: necessary, small, and *not* the growth line.
- **Spend the citation budget on rank and visible facts.** Position decides citation fate
  (43% at rank 1 → 5% at rank 7; Fischman, 2026). Content that earns the slot — the
  evidence-led levers covered in the
  [hub article](/resources/research/ai-citation-levers-2026) — is what rank is made of.
- **Treat every vendor "schema +39%" pitch as a correlation claim.** The GEO-16 figure is
  an artifact of citation-harvested sampling; the controlled RCT the vendors don't quote
  came back null or negative. Ask for their controlled n, not their correlation.

If you're measuring this rather than litigating it, the
[citation-intelligence buyer's guide](/resources/research/ai-visibility-measurement-buyers-guide)
covers how to track named mentions without fooling yourself with a noisy week — and the
[AI crawler readiness check](/resources/research/ai-crawlers-2026-readiness) covers why
your schema matters less than whether the engine can reach your page in the first place.
